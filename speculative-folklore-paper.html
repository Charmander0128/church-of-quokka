Rest, Kindness and Comic Ritual: A Speculative Folklore Study of Quokkism

Church of Quokka Editorial Collective · Creative paper, Version 1.0 · 2026

Abstract

This creative paper examines Quokkism as an openly invented internet belief system founded in 2026. It asks how the forms of religion—scripture, ritual, office, symbol and community—can be used satirically to express ordinary values such as rest, kindness and tolerance of uncertainty. The analysis treats the Church’s own fictional pages as primary creative texts, not as evidence of an older tradition.

1. Introduction

Internet communities often borrow ceremonial language to make everyday ideas memorable. Quokkism uses the public image of the smiling quokka as a comic emblem: a small creature apparently meeting an unreasonable universe with composure. The joke works only when its invention is visible.

2. Method

The method is close reading of the project’s published worldbuilding: the Book of Leaves, the Five Commandments, the Eternal Nap and the fictional office of Pope Quokka I. No interviews, ethnographic fieldwork or claims about Indigenous knowledge were used.

3. Findings

3.1 Rest as counter-ritual

The Eternal Nap turns rest from a reward into a playful moral right. Its mock-sacred framing criticises productivity culture without demanding literal belief.

3.2 Kindness without grandeur

The quokka symbol keeps the project deliberately small. Quokkism’s imagined authority is repeatedly undercut by leaves, naps and paw prints, allowing gentleness to appear without solemnity.

3.3 Smiling into uncertainty

The phrase “smile into the abyss” does not deny difficulty. It proposes humour as one possible response to uncertainty, while leaving room for sadness, seriousness and disagreement.

4. Ethical boundary

The quokka is native to Western Australia, where Country and cultural heritage belong to living Aboriginal communities. Geographic association does not give this project permission to invent Indigenous ancestry. Quokkism therefore identifies itself as a 2026 creative work and directs readers seeking cultural history to Indigenous-led and official sources.

5. Conclusion

Quokkism is best understood as participatory satire: a fictional framework through which people can make art, exchange kindness and question needless seriousness. Its cultural value, if any develops, must come from transparent contemporary participation—not a manufactured past.
